Before anything elseYou do not have to count calories forever. Most people who succeed count carefully for a few weeks, learn roughly what their food is worth, and then stop. This page is here to teach you that, not to turn eating into arithmetic for the rest of your life.
QUESTION 1

"How much am I supposed to be eating?"

There is a number, and you can work it out in thirty seconds. But understand what it is: an educated starting guess, accurate to within about 10%. Your bathroom scale over two weeks will tell you more than any formula ever could.
Work out your daily target
Why this number is adjusted for youStandard calorie formulas were built on Western populations. Research by ICMR-NIN found they overestimate Indian calorie needs by roughly 10%, because Indians tend to carry proportionally more fat and less muscle at the same height and weight. This calculator applies that correction. Most apps do not, which is one reason people eat more than they think they should.
QWhat if I eat less than the number — will I lose faster?
For a short while. Then it backfires, reliably.Very large deficits cause muscle loss, constant hunger, low energy, poor sleep, and a mood that makes you quit. Most crash diets fail not because people lack discipline, but because the plan was never survivable.

Aim to lose about 0.5–1% of your body weight per week. For an 80 kg person, that is 400–800 g. Slower than you want. Faster than the alternative, which is starting over every three months.
Absolute minimum per dayWhy you should not go below
Women1200 kcalBelow this it becomes almost impossible to get enough protein, iron, calcium and B vitamins from food.
Men1500 kcalSame problem, plus rapid muscle loss and a metabolism that adapts downward.

QUESTION 2

"Do I have to weigh everything?"

No. And most people who try give up within a fortnight. Your hand is a measuring device you always have with you, it scales to your body size, and it is accurate enough for almost everyone.

How to use thisA typical meal for a man: 2 palms of protein, 2 cupped hands of carbs, 2 thumbs of fat, 2 fists of vegetables. For a woman, halve the first three. Eat that at three or four meals and you will land close to a sensible target without counting a single calorie.

Losing weight? Drop one cupped hand of carbs and one thumb of fat per meal. Gaining? Add them back.

Calibrate your katori — this matters more than you think

The single most useful thing on this pageA katori is anywhere from 120 ml to 200 ml depending on whose kitchen it came from. That is a 60-calorie swing on rice alone, three times a day.

Do this once: fill your usual katori with water, pour it into a measuring cup, and write the number on the bottom with a marker. Everything below assumes a 150 ml katori. If yours is 120 ml, reduce these numbers by 20%. If 200 ml, add a third.

QUESTION 4

"Am I getting enough protein?"

Almost certainly not, if you eat a typical Indian vegetarian diet. This is the most common gap by a wide margin — and the one that most affects how you look, how full you feel, and how much muscle you keep while losing fat.
QHow much protein do I actually need?
About 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight, if you train.For a 70 kg person that is roughly 110 to 155 g per day. If you do not train at all, 0.8–1.2 g/kg is adequate for health — but the higher range helps with fullness and muscle regardless.

Why it matters more than anything else on this page: protein keeps you full, costs your body 20–30% of its own calories just to digest, and protects your muscle while you lose fat. Get this right and a lot of other things stop mattering as much.

The dal problemLook at that table again. Four katoris of dal to reach 30 g of protein — at 580 calories. One palm of chicken does it for 165. For vegetarians, 30 g of soya chunks does it for 100.

Dal is a good food and should stay in your diet. But if you are relying on dal as your protein source, you are eating a great many calories for not much protein. This is the single biggest structural problem with Indian vegetarian eating, and it is entirely fixable.

You may have heard

Vegetarians must carefully combine proteins at every meal.

What is actually true

This was believed in the 1970s and has since been abandoned. Your body maintains a pool of amino acids and draws on it across the day. Eating a variety of plant proteins across the day is entirely sufficient — you do not need to engineer each meal. Traditional Indian combinations like dal chawal happen to do this anyway.

QDo I need protein powder?
No. But it is genuinely useful if you struggle to hit your target.It is food in a convenient form, not a drug and not a shortcut. One scoop gives roughly 24 g of protein for 120 calories, which is hard to match with whole food.

Try food first. If after two weeks of honest effort you are still 30 g short every day, a scoop solves it cheaply. If you are hitting your target with food, save your money.
QUESTION 5

"Is this food good or bad for me?"

Wrong question. Almost no single food is good or bad — the amount and the frequency are what matter. Ghee is not poison. Salad is not magic. A diet is the sum of what you do most days, not a moral test you pass or fail at each meal.
How oftenWhat belongs hereThe point
Every day, freelyVegetables, dal, curd, eggs, fruit, whole grains, milkBuild the base of your eating from these and the rest sorts itself out.
Every day, measuredOil, ghee, nuts, rice, roti, paneerNecessary and good — but the quantity is what decides your weekly total.
A few times a weekSweets, fried snacks, restaurant meals, alcoholNot forbidden. Just not daily.
Occasionally, and enjoy itWhatever you genuinely loveA diet with no room for celebration food is a diet you will quit.

How to read a label in ten seconds

Look at thisWhat to checkRed flag
1. Serving size — first, alwaysEverything else on the label refers to thisA 200-calorie packet that says "2 servings" is a 400-calorie packet.
2. Calories per 100 gLets you compare two products fairlyOver 400 per 100 g means calorie-dense. Fine occasionally, easy to overeat.
3. Protein per 100 gThe number that decides whether it fills you upUnder 5 g per 100 g in a "protein" product is marketing.
4. Sugar — the "of which sugars" lineNot total carbs; the sugar lineOver 15 g per 100 g is a sweet, whatever the front of the packet claims.
5. The ingredient listListed by weight, highest firstIf sugar (or any of its aliases) is in the first three, it is a sweet.
6. Ignore the front of the packet entirelyIt is advertising, not information"Natural", "made with real fruit", "high in fibre" have no fixed meaning.

QUESTION 6

"Why has the scale not moved?"

Because body weight is not the same thing as body fat, and it moves for a dozen reasons that have nothing to do with fat. You can lose fat for three weeks and see the scale go up. This is normal and it stops almost everyone.
Why the scale jumpedHow much it can moveHow long it lasts
Salty meal the night before0.5 – 1.5 kg1–3 days
More carbs than usual0.5 – 2 kg2–4 days — each gram of stored carb holds about 3 g of water
A hard new workout0.5 – 1 kg2–4 days — muscle holds water while it repairs
Not having been to the toilet0.3 – 1 kgHours
Period (women)0.5 – 2 kgUp to a week
Poor sleep or high stress0.3 – 1 kgDays — cortisol holds water
Alcohol the night before0.5 – 1 kg1–2 days
Actual fat gain~0.1 kg per day at mostYou cannot gain 2 kg of fat overnight. It is physically impossible.
How to weigh yourself properlyWeigh every morning — after the toilet, before eating or drinking, wearing the same thing.
Write it down and ignore it. Individual days are meaningless.
At the end of each week, average the seven numbers. Compare that average to last week’s average.

That single change — comparing weekly averages instead of daily readings — removes almost all the frustration from tracking weight.

If it genuinely has not moved in three weeks

Check thisHow to check itMost common finding
1. Has it really been 3+ weeks?Compare weekly averages, not single daysTwo flat weeks is normal noise, not a plateau.
2. Are you weighing the oil?Measure cooking oil with a spoon for one weekThe most common single cause. Free-pouring can add 300+ calories a day.
3. Are you counting weekends?Log Saturday and Sunday honestlyTwo heavy days can erase five good ones.
4. Are you counting drinks?Chai, cold drinks, juice, alcoholFive sugared chais = 450 calories that nobody logs.
5. Have you started moving less?Check your step count against a month agoPeople unconsciously move less when eating less. Very common.
6. Are you sleeping?Under 6 hours raises hunger and lowers willpowerFix sleep before cutting calories further.
7. Have you lost a lot already?A smaller body burns fewer caloriesAfter 10%+ loss, recalculate your target.
QUESTION 7

"Do I need vitamins and supplements?"

Mostly you need food. But there are three or four things that Indian diets genuinely tend to run short on, and they are worth knowing about rather than buying a multivitamin and hoping.
NutrientWhat it doesWhere to get it in an Indian kitchenWho tends to run short
Vitamin DBone strength, muscle function, immunity, moodSunlight (15–20 min), fortified milk, egg yolk, mushroomAlmost everyone with an indoor job. Worth getting tested.
Vitamin B12Nerves, red blood cells, energyOnly animal foods: milk, curd, paneer, egg, meat, fishLong-term vegetarians and vegans — nearly always. Supplement is usually needed.
IronCarries oxygen. Low iron = constant tirednessGreen leafy veg, dal, jaggery, dates, red meatWomen, especially with heavy periods. Very common in India.
CalciumBones, teeth, muscle contractionMilk, curd, paneer, ragi, sesame, drumstick leavesPeople who avoid dairy

The three worth actually testing forVitamin D — deficiency is close to universal among people with indoor jobs.
Vitamin B12 — if you have been vegetarian for years, you are very likely low. It causes nerve damage silently.

A blood test costs less than three months of guessing. Ask a doctor — do not start supplementing iron or high-dose vitamins on your own, as too much of either causes real harm.

QShould I just take a multivitamin?
It will not hurt, and it is not a substitute for food.A basic multivitamin is cheap insurance against small gaps. It will not fix a genuine deficiency — the doses are usually too low — and it will not compensate for a poor diet.

If you suspect a real deficiency, test rather than guess. Treating low iron or low B12 needs proper doses under medical guidance, not a generic tablet.
QUESTION 8

"Does when I eat matter?"

Far less than you have been told. Almost every rule you have heard about meal timing is either marketing or a misreading of a small study. Here are the honest answers.
QuestionShort answerDetail
Does eating late make me fat?NoYour body does not check the clock. Total intake over the day and week decides it. Late eating correlates with overeating — that is the real mechanism.
Is breakfast the most important meal?NoMarketing, largely. If you are hungry in the morning, eat. If not, do not force it.
Do I need to eat every 3 hours?NoMeal frequency has almost no effect on metabolic rate. Eat in whatever pattern keeps you full and consistent.
Should I eat before training?Depends on youSomething light 1–2 hours before helps most people. Some train fine fasted. Try both; no wrong answer.
Do I need protein within 30 minutes after?No — that window is hours wideYour muscles stay responsive for around 24 hours. Daily total matters far more than timing.
Does intermittent fasting work?Yes, but not magicallyIt works because eating in a shorter window usually means eating less. If it suits you, use it. If it makes you binge at 8pm, it is the wrong tool.
How much water?Roughly 2–3 litres, more when hot or trainingPale yellow urine is the simplest check. Dark = drink more. Completely clear = you can ease off.

"How do I do this in real life?"

This is where most plans die. Not at the calculation, not at the grocery shop — at the wedding, the office celebration, and the relative who is genuinely hurt that you did not take a second helping.

The 80/20 idea, stated properlyIf 80% of your eating is reasonably sensible — enough protein, plenty of vegetables, controlled oil, not too much sugar — the other 20% genuinely does not matter.

That is not permission to be sloppy. It is permission to stop treating every meal as a test. The people who succeed long term are not the strictest. They are the ones who never needed to start over.
QUESTION 10

"Is that thing I read online true?"

Almost never. Here are the ones that cause the most wasted effort and unnecessary guilt.
You may have heard

Carbs make you fat. Cut rice and roti.

What is actually true

Excess calories make you fat, from any source. Entire populations have eaten rice as a staple for millennia without an obesity problem. Carbohydrates are your body’s preferred fuel for training. Control the portion — one katori rather than two cups — and keep the food.

You may have heard

Eating fat makes you fat.

What is actually true

Fat is essential for hormones, brain function and absorbing vitamins A, D, E and K. The catch is that it is 9 calories per gram versus 4 for carbs and protein, so it is very easy to overeat without noticing. The problem is never the ghee itself — it is the ghee you did not measure.

You may have heard

You need to detox or cleanse.

What is actually true

Your liver and kidneys detoxify your blood continuously, every second, for free. No juice, tea or powder improves on them. What "detox" programmes actually do is restrict calories for a few days, which is why people lose water weight and feel briefly virtuous.

You may have heard

Some foods burn fat. Green tea, lemon water, chilli.

What is actually true

The effects are real but tiny — a handful of calories a day, well within measurement error. Green tea is a nice drink. Lemon water is pleasant. Neither will move your weight. Anyone selling you a fat-burning food is selling you something.

You may have heard

Eating small frequent meals speeds up your metabolism.

What is actually true

Meal frequency has essentially no effect on metabolic rate. Six meals and two meals of the same total burn the same. Eat in whatever pattern keeps you full and consistent — that is the only criterion that matters.

You may have heard

Gluten-free and sugar-free are healthier.

What is actually true

Unless you have coeliac disease, removing gluten offers no proven benefit, and gluten-free products are often higher in sugar and fat to compensate for texture. "Sugar-free" biscuits contain the same refined flour, the same fat, and roughly the same calories.

You may have heard

You must eat protein immediately after training or you wasted the session.

What is actually true

The so-called anabolic window is hours wide, not thirty minutes. Your muscles stay responsive to protein for around a day afterwards. Hit your daily protein target and the timing sorts itself out. Nobody has ever lost gains walking home from the gym.

You may have heard

Ghee is a superfood. / Ghee is poison.

What is actually true

Neither. It is a fat: 45 calories per teaspoon, useful for cooking, pleasant to eat, and entirely fine in measured amounts. It is not medicine and it will not raise your metabolism. Measure it with a spoon and stop arguing about it.

You may have heard

Fruit has too much sugar.

What is actually true

Fruit comes packaged with fibre, water and micronutrients, which is why it fills you up and does not spike blood sugar the way juice or sweets do. Almost nobody in recorded history has become overweight from eating too much fruit.

You may have heard

You have to give up everything you enjoy.

What is actually true

The diets that work are the ones people can live with. A plan that removes every food you love has a predictable ending, and you already know what it is. Build the base well and leave deliberate room for the food that makes life good.

The whole thing, in six lines1. Work out your calorie number, then trust the scale over the formula.
2. Get your protein right — a palm at every meal. This is the one that changes everything.
3. Measure your oil with a spoon.
4. Fill half the plate with vegetables.
5. Compare weekly averages, never single days.
6. Leave room for the food you love, or you will quit.

That is genuinely the whole thing. Everything above is detail supporting those six lines.
Where these numbers come from. Food values are based on the Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT 2017) from ICMR-NIN, adjusted to typical home-cooked portions and household measures. The calorie calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation with the ICMR-NIN correction for Indian body composition, which the 2020 expert group found necessary because international formulas overestimate Indian requirements by around 10%. Portion guidance follows the hand-size method developed by Precision Nutrition. Protein recommendations follow the International Society of Sports Nutrition and current meta-analyses.

A note on accuracy. Every food figure here is an estimate. Cooking oil, portion size and recipe vary enormously between kitchens. These numbers are close enough to make good decisions with — they are not laboratory measurements, and they do not need to be.

Please note. This is general information, not personal dietary or medical advice. If you have diabetes, kidney disease, thyroid problems, are pregnant, or have any history of disordered eating, speak to a doctor or registered dietitian before changing how you eat.
