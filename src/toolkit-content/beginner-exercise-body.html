QUESTION 1

"I want to start, but I have no idea what to do."

The honest answer: almost any sensible plan works if you actually do it. The best programme in the world is worthless if you quit in three weeks. So pick the simplest thing you will genuinely stick to.
QHow many days a week should I train?
Two or three, if you are starting out.More is not better at the beginning — it is just harder to sustain. Two full-body sessions a week produces most of the strength and health benefit available to a beginner. Once you have done three months without missing much, then consider adding a fourth day.

The rule: choose the number of days you would still manage in a bad week, not a good one.
QHow long should each session be?
30 to 45 minutes is plenty.A focused 35-minute session beats a distracted 90-minute one. If you are in the gym for two hours, most of it is probably phone time.

Short on time? Ten minutes twice a week is genuinely worth doing. Not optimal — but far closer to optimal than zero.

QUESTION 2

"How hard am I supposed to be working?"

This is the question nobody answers properly. You do not need a heart rate monitor or a app. You need two simple tests: can you still talk, and how many reps did you have left.
QHow do I know if a weight is right for me?
If you could have done 2 or 3 more reps, it is about right.Not "did it feel hard" — that changes with your mood, sleep and coffee. Ask instead: how many more could I have done?

If the answer is zero, it was too heavy. If it is five or more, too light. Two or three is the sweet spot for almost every set you will ever do.

You may have heard

If you are not sore, the workout did not work.

What is actually true

Soreness mostly reflects doing something new, not something effective. Experienced lifters get very little soreness and keep making progress. Chasing soreness leads to bad training decisions.

QUESTION 3

"How many sets and reps? And how long do I rest?"

There is no magic number. There are sensible ranges, and the differences between them matter far less than most people online will tell you.

QHow long should I rest between sets?
Longer than you think. 60 to 90 seconds for most things.Beginners rush. If your next set suffers because you were still breathing hard, you rested too little.

Heavy strength work: 2–3 minutes, and yes, that feels like a long time.
Building muscle: 60–90 seconds.
Conditioning: 30–45 seconds, deliberately short.

The simplest test: rest until you can breathe normally and genuinely believe you can hit your reps again.
QWhat is the least I can get away with?
Two sessions a week, 20 minutes each. Really.This is not a compromise position — it is a genuinely defensible one. The gap between nothing and a little is far larger than the gap between a little and a lot.

Most of the health benefit of exercise arrives in the first few hours per week. Everything beyond that is for performance and appearance, which are fine goals — but do not let them stop you starting small.

QUESTION 4

"How do I actually keep getting better?"

One idea matters more than everything else in this document combined. It is called progressive overload, and it simply means: over time, do a little more than you did before. That is it. That is the whole secret.
The only rule you truly needIf you do the same workout with the same weights forever, your body has no reason to change. Do slightly more than last time — one more rep, or a little more weight — and your body adapts. Everything else in training is a detail on top of this.

Six ways to do "a little more"

Way to progressWhat it looks likeWhen to use it
1. Add repsLast week 3×8. This week 3×9 with the same weight.Easiest. Use this first, every time.
2. Add weightHit the top of your range → add 2.5–5 kg → drop back to the bottom.When the top of your rep range feels manageable.
3. Add a set3 sets became 4.When you have time and are recovering well.
4. Better formDeeper squat. Slower lowering. No bouncing.Always. Free progress that costs nothing.
5. Shorter rest90 seconds became 60.For conditioning. Not for strength.
6. Harder versionKnee push-up → full push-up → feet elevated.Bodyweight training, where you cannot add plates.

Planning three months — without complicating it

Coaches call this periodisation. All it really means is: you cannot push hard forever, so plan the easy weeks instead of collapsing into them.
WeeksWhat you focus onHow it should feelWhat changes
1–4Learn the movements. Light weight.Easy. You will feel you could do more.Add reps each week. Do not chase weight yet.
5–8Add weight steadily.Properly working. 2–3 reps left in the tank.Small weight jumps most weeks.
9–11Push a bit harder.Hard. 1–2 reps left on your last set.Progress slows. Normal.
12Deload — cut everything roughly in half.Almost too easy. That is the point.You come back stronger. Skip this and you stall.
Why the easy week is not optionalWeek 12 looks like wasted time. It is the opposite. Training breaks your body down; rest is when it rebuilds stronger. Skip the easy weeks and you eventually stop progressing, get injured, or lose motivation — usually all three.

Steps — what they are worth

Steps to calories
You may have heard

You must hit 10,000 steps a day.

What is actually true

The 10,000 figure came from a 1960s Japanese pedometer marketing campaign, not from science. Health benefits rise steeply from around 4,000 steps and keep improving to roughly 8,000–10,000. If you currently do 3,000, getting to 6,000 is a genuine win. Aim to beat your own average, not a round number someone invented to sell a device.

QCardio or weights for fat loss?
Weights for the body you want. Diet for the fat loss. Cardio for your heart.Cardio burns more calories per session. Weights preserve the muscle that makes you look and feel better as the fat comes off — and keep your metabolism higher.

If you can only do one: lift weights, and walk a lot.
If you can do both: lift 2–3 times a week, walk daily, add some harder cardio if you enjoy it.

Notice that neither answer is "spend an hour on the treadmill every day."

"How long until I see results?"

Longer than social media implies, and sooner than you fear. The most important thing to understand: the changes you can feel arrive long before the changes you can see.

You may have heard

What is actually true

Body weight swings by 1–2 kg in a day from water, salt, food still in your gut, and hormones. You can lose fat and gain muscle and see the scale not move at all — while looking and feeling completely different. Weigh yourself often, but only ever compare weekly averages.

QUESTION 8

"Is that thing I heard actually true?"

Almost certainly not. Here are the ones that cause the most wasted effort.
You may have heard

Doing sit-ups will burn the fat on my stomach.

What is actually true

Spot reduction does not exist. You cannot choose where fat comes off — that is decided by genetics and hormones. Ab exercises build the muscle underneath; the fat on top comes off through overall fat loss. This is why people with visible abs are lean, not why lean people do lots of crunches.

You may have heard

Lifting weights will make women bulky.

What is actually true

Building large amounts of muscle is extremely difficult and takes years of deliberate effort and eating. Women have roughly a tenth to a fifteenth of the testosterone men do. What lifting actually produces is the look most people mean when they say "toned" — which is simply muscle, with less fat on top.

You may have heard

If I stop training, my muscle turns to fat.

What is actually true

Muscle and fat are completely different tissues. One cannot become the other, any more than a bone can become blood. What happens is that muscle shrinks from disuse while fat increases from eating the same as before — two separate things happening at once, which looks like a transformation.

You may have heard

Sweating a lot means I burned more fat.

What is actually true

Sweat is temperature regulation, not fat loss. A hot room, high humidity or simply being a heavy sweater will all soak your shirt without burning an extra calorie. The weight you lose during a session is water — you regain it the moment you drink.

You may have heard

No pain, no gain.

What is actually true

Discomfort and effort are part of training. Pain is a signal to stop. Confusing the two is how people get injured and end up unable to train for months. Learn the difference: muscles ache, joints hurt. Never train through joint pain.

You may have heard

Fasted cardio burns more fat.

What is actually true

You burn slightly more fat during the session, and correspondingly less afterwards. Over 24 hours it evens out. Total calories still decide fat loss. Train fasted if you prefer it — just do not expect a bonus.

You may have heard

Machines are for beginners, free weights are better.

What is actually true

Machines are excellent. They are easier to learn, safer to fail on alone, and let you work a muscle hard without needing perfect technique first. Plenty of very strong, very muscular people use them extensively. Use whichever you will actually use.

You may have heard

I am too old to start.

What is actually true

Strength training becomes more important with age, not less. People in their seventies and eighties build meaningful muscle and strength in supervised programmes. Muscle mass and strength are among the strongest predictors of independence and quality of life in later years. Starting late beats not starting.

You may have heard

I need supplements to see results.

What is actually true

You need consistent training, enough protein, and enough sleep. Nothing sold in a tub replaces any of those. Creatine and whey protein are genuinely useful and well studied — but they are a small refinement on top of the basics, worth perhaps a few percent. Everything else in the shop is mostly optional.

The whole thing, in five lines1. Train 2–3 times a week, whole body, for 30–45 minutes.
2. Pick weights where you finish a set with 2–3 reps still in you.
3. Do slightly more than last time, whenever you can.
4. Walk a lot, sleep properly, eat enough protein.
5. Take an easy week roughly every 8–12 weeks.

That is it. That is the whole game. Everything else you will read about is a footnote to those five lines.
Where these numbers come from. Calorie figures are calculated from the 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities, the reference database used by exercise scientists. Heart rate zones use the Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7 × age), which is more accurate than the older 220 − age, particularly over 40. A note on safety. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, diabetes, are pregnant, or have any injury or medical condition, speak to your doctor before starting. If you get chest pain, severe breathlessness or feel faint during exercise, stop immediately and seek medical help.
